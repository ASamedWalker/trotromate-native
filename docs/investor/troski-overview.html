Trotro,
made legible.

Eight in ten motorised trips in Accra happen on a system with no schedule, no published fare, and no map.

Troski is the digital layer for that system — telling a rider what a trip should cost, where the vehicle leaves from, and how long the queue is, on the phones and data budgets people actually have.

The problem

The network everyone uses, and nobody can see

The trotro — a shared minibus that departs when it fills — carries up to 86% of Accra's residents and around 80% of the city's motorised trips. It has no timetable, because vehicles leave when full rather than at a time. It has no published fare list online: prices are negotiated nationally as a single percentage change, then posted as printed charts at each lorry station. Boarding order is kept in a paper ledger by the station "bookman".

So the gap isn't vehicles — Accra has plenty. The gap is certainty. A rider stepping onto an unfamiliar route doesn't know the fare, can't tell whether they're being overcharged, and has no way to compare one station's queue against another's. After each fare announcement, even regular riders are unsure what they now owe, and unions must police drivers charging above the approved rate.

Why it is still unsolved

Better-funded attempts have already failed here

  • The data decays faster than it can be collected

    Routes shift constantly. WhereIsMyTransport raised roughly $27M mapping African transport and shut down in 2023, unable to make collection economics work. The canonical open dataset for Accra's routes is frozen at September 2017 and has had no refresh pipeline since.

  • There is no schedule to publish

    Apps built on timetable assumptions import stale 2017 route codes and present them as live service. The model doesn't fit the system, so the output is confidently wrong.

  • The union is the operating system

    Fares, queue order and route allocation run through GPRTU branches and their bookmen. Technology that routes around them gets rejected — the union said publicly of one cashless scheme that it "can never work as trotro."

  • Riders are on cheap phones and expensive data

    A product that assumes live maps and constant connectivity prices out the people it serves. This is a hard design constraint, not a polish item.

The insight

Fares move as one number, nationwide

Ghana doesn't reprice route by route. The union negotiates a single national percentage — fares rose 20% in June 2026; a proposed 30% was suspended in July when government cut diesel prices — and every station updates its printed chart by that same figure.

That is the opening. It means fare data does not have to be re-surveyed each time it changes. Collect a baseline from the charts once, then apply one percentage and the entire network is current again. The recurring cost that killed everyone else becomes a few minutes of work per announcement.

The product

Four ways in, two networks, one dataset underneath

Troski meets people where they already are, rather than assuming everyone will install an app. Every surface reads from and writes to the same route, fare and station data.

Rider · mobile app

Plan a trip, see the fare to your specific drop-off rather than one flat corridor price, check station queues, buy a ticket and pay by mobile money.

Rider · WhatsApp

Ask a fare in plain language — English, Twi, Ga, Pidgin — with no install and almost no data. The lowest-friction door into the network.

Rider · rail (web + app)

All three of Ghana's passenger lines — Tema–Accra, Tema–Mpakadan, Sekondi–Takoradi — with official times, station lists, live countdowns to the next departure and reminders before it leaves.

Operator · Troski Pro

A driver and fleet-owner app: shift tracking, cash and mobile-money takings, vehicle location, and the first real utilisation data owners have ever had.

The rail product is worth singling out, because it does three jobs at once. It is the cheapest channel we have — Ghana train queries are the largest source of organic search demand reaching us, and they cost nothing to win. It is proof the model works against an official operator: the fares shown are the rail authority's own, including the zone pricing on Tema–Mpakadan, and the data carries the date it was last checked, so a stale schedule is visible rather than silently wrong. And it is a second network running on the same spine, which is what makes this a transport platform rather than a trotro app.

It is also deliberately restrained: rail is information only. We show what a ticket costs and when the train goes, but we don't sell the ticket, because we don't have the right to. That waits on an agreement with the rail authority — the same discipline we apply to the trotro unions, and the reason both parties can treat us as a partner rather than a threat.

The operator side matters more than it looks. Drivers rent their vehicle for a fixed daily amount and keep the rest, so owners have no visibility into what a route actually earns — and drivers carry a day of cash, which is why the union itself wants to go cashless.

Where it goes

Information first, because it earns the right to the rest

Now

Be the answer to "what does this cost?"

Own fare truth and freshness across Accra. Trust is the asset; everything after this depends on riders and unions believing the number.

Next

Move the money

Ticketing and mobile-money fare payment, with the driver app closing the loop. This is where revenue begins, and it addresses the robbery risk the union already wants solved.

Later

Sell certainty to the operators

Route-level demand and revenue data no union or owner has today — informing fleet deployment, and positioning us as the matchmaker for electric minibus adoption rather than the balance sheet that owns the vehicles.

Why now

Three clocks are running

  • The union is shopping. GPRTU has publicly said it wants a cashless system to stop drivers being robbed for their daily cash — and has no named technology partner.
  • Electric buses have a deadline. Ghana's import-duty waiver for public-transport EVs runs to 2032, which sets the window for the fleet-enablement phase.
  • Competitors have noticed. Two crowdsourced-fare apps launched in 2026. The difference is refresh: they collect fares, they don't have a mechanism to keep them true.

Where we are. Troski is founder-built and early, but it is not a prototype: the rider app has been published on the App Store and Google Play since February 2026 and ships regular updates. The WhatsApp assistant, the rail product and the operator app are built and working, and the fare pipeline is in place and being populated from union and station charts. We are pre-revenue, and this is an introduction to the concept rather than a fundraise document — happy to walk through the product, the data model, or the union strategy in detail.
